# BeMama social content — Week 1 drafts

Status: draft only. Do not publish until reviewed.

Audience: pregnant people, new parents, breastfeeding/pumping parents, combination-feeding families, and their support network.

Voice: warm, practical, non-judgmental, lightly humorous. BeMama is an organization and support tool, not a replacement for a pediatrician, midwife, lactation consultant, or other qualified clinician.

## Trend-informed direction

The strongest content opportunity is not another perfect-mom checklist. It is useful relief around the things parents are actively discussing:

- Pumping output, freezer-stash comparison, pump gear, and the pressure to optimize every ounce.
- Returning to work while pumping and the practical problems of cleaning, storing, and transporting milk.
- Breast, bottle, formula, and combination feeding without shame or “good mother” scorekeeping.
- Responsive feeding: reading hunger and fullness cues instead of treating the clock or bottle volume as the whole story.
- Starting solids without internet pressure or unsafe shortcuts.
- The invisible mental load: remembering feeds, pump parts, appointments, supplies, and who needs what next.

The angle for BeMama: make parents feel less alone and more organized, while clearly separating general education from individual medical advice.

### Day 2 — Carousel: “Pumping at work: a gentler plan”

Visual: `content/social/week-1/visuals/pumping-planning-watercolor.png`

Slides:

1. “Returning to work and pumping? Start with a plan—not pressure.”
2. “Practice before the first workday.” Give yourself time to learn your pump and let baby become familiar with the planned feeding method.
3. “Think in routines.” Plan pumping around the times baby usually feeds, while leaving room for real life and your own comfort.
4. “Pack the boring essentials.” Clean containers or storage bags, labels, a cooler if needed, spare pump parts, and a way to clean parts.
5. “Know your storage guidance.” Use current CDC guidance for time, temperature, and handling; local workplace and childcare rules may differ.
6. “You do not have to solve the whole month today.” Put the next feed, pump, appointment, or reminder somewhere you can find it.
7. “Save this. Share it with the person helping you plan.”

Caption:

> A return-to-work feeding plan should make the day more manageable—not turn you into a milk-production machine. CDC guidance recommends practicing pumping ahead of time, planning for clean storage, and learning what support is available at work. Details vary by baby, health situation, workplace, and local guidance, so check with your clinician when needed.
>
> What would make pumping at work easier for you: time, privacy, storage, cleaning, or support?

Source note: CDC pumping and breast-milk storage guidance.

### Day 6 — Carousel: “Responsive feeding: cues before the clock”

Visual: `content/social/week-1/visuals/feeding-bond-watercolor.png`

Slides:

1. “Before the crying: look for the early cues.”
2. “Hungry may look like…” hands moving to mouth, rooting, lip smacking, becoming more alert, or turning toward breast or bottle.
3. “Full may look like…” slowing or stopping, turning away, relaxing, or losing interest.
4. “This applies to breast and bottle feeding.” The goal is to respond to the baby—not win a numbers argument.
5. “A schedule may be medically important for some babies.” Premature babies and babies with health concerns need the plan their clinician gives them.
6. “Save the cues. Share them with every caregiver.”

Caption:

> Responsive feeding means noticing and responding to hunger and fullness cues. It can help caregivers focus on the baby in front of them instead of comparing every feed to a chart online. If your baby was premature, is not growing as expected, or has a medical condition, follow the care plan from your clinician.
>
> Which cue did you learn only after becoming a parent?

Source note: CDC/AAP responsive-feeding guidance.

### Day 7 — Carousel: “Starting solids without internet pressure”

Visual: `C:/WorkSpace/Practice/Media/Characters/4.png`

Slides:

1. “Starting solids? Readiness matters more than trends.”
2. “Look for signs such as…” good head control, sitting with support, reaching for food, and less pushing food out with the tongue.
3. “Milk is still important.” Complementary foods add to milk feeds; they do not replace the whole feeding relationship overnight.
4. “Keep it simple and safe.” Follow age-appropriate texture and food-safety guidance, and ask your clinician about allergy or medical concerns.
5. “Do not force a bite.” Mealtimes are practice, learning, and connection.
6. “Skip internet shortcuts.” No cereal in a bottle, no miracle food, and no pressure to copy another baby’s timeline.
7. “Save this for the week you begin.”

Caption:

> The internet can make starting solids feel like a performance. WHO guidance places complementary feeding around 6 months and emphasizes safe, adequate foods alongside continued breastfeeding where chosen. Readiness and medical needs vary—ask your pediatrician or dietitian for personalized advice.
>
> What is the first food your family is curious about?

Source note: WHO complementary-feeding guidance and AAP readiness guidance.

## Comment-response prompts

Use real replies instead of automated or repetitive comments:

- “That sounds exhausting. What part of the routine is hardest right now?”
- “Are you looking for general planning ideas or a question to take to your clinician?”
- “Would a printable checklist or a reminder inside BeMama help most?”
- “Thank you for sharing a different feeding experience—families need more than one valid path.”

## Safety and moderation checklist

- Add “general education, not medical advice” to feeding posts where appropriate.
- Do not give a target ounce amount, diagnose low supply, recommend supplements, or promise a way to increase milk.
- Do not shame formula, bottle, pumping, nursing, or combination feeding.
- Do not show unsafe sleep or feeding positions.
- Remove comments that pressure parents to ignore a clinician or replace professional care with an internet tip.
- Use current CDC, WHO, and AAP guidance when a post includes storage, safety, age, or medical details.
